Why

The person who matters most in a ROAM room is the one who is not in the home: they joined from somewhere else, and the scanned home is the only way they can be there. Today they move by holding W/A/S/D and dragging a mouse. On a phone they cannot move at all, because there are no keys to hold. On a laptop the view stutters: measured on the owner's own, the scan draws at 2 to 28 frames a second, so the home it is meant to show is a slideshow.

The person standing in the real home needs none of that. They can see the home with their eyes. What the room needs from them is their camera and where they are standing, which is what their phone already sends.

So this change is about moving through the room, for whoever is away from it: a way to walk on every device, a view that keeps up on the machine it is on, and — on an Android phone away from the home — walking the scan by walking your own floor.

What Changes

  1. 1
    Joined from anywhere on an Android phone, you walk the home by walking your own floor. Press Walk it in the room and you are inside the scan: turn your body to look, take a step and you take a step in the home, at life size. When you run out of real floor, one press lets you carry on walking from where you got to. Everyone else in the room sees your figure walk as you do, with nothing new to set up.

  2. 2
    Every phone in a room can finally move. A thumb pad sits in the corner of the view: press it to walk, drag anywhere else to look. Today a phone guest can only look around from one spot, because moving needs keys a phone does not have. The pad is there whether or not the phone can do the walking version.

  3. 3
    The view keeps up on the machine you are on, whatever it is. Measured on the owner's own laptop, the scanned home drew at 2 to 28 frames a second — the plan had assumed ninety, from a different machine. So the view now watches its own frame rate and gives up detail until it holds: full detail where that works, fewer and larger splats where it does not. Nobody is asked to choose, and the softening is barely visible beside the stutter it replaces.

  4. 4
    On a laptop, leaning shifts the view, like leaning towards a window. Turn on Lean to look and the laptop's own camera watches your head: lean left and you see a little further round the left of the doorway. It is a small, calm effect that makes the room feel like a place rather than a picture — not a way to get around, so the keys and the mouse stay exactly as they are. It starts off, because it uses the camera and some of the laptop's effort.

  5. 5
    The phone in the real home keeps doing exactly what it does today. It sends its camera and where it is standing, and draws nothing. It was going to show the scanned home over the real one; that is dropped, because somebody standing in the home can already see the home. The note screen is untouched.

Non-goals: the phone in the home drawing anything, turning a drawing on and off there, notes seen as markers over the real room, an iPhone walking by its own movement (its browser cannot tell where it is — it gets the thumb pad), leaning as a way to travel rather than to peer, making a step cover more ground than a step, drawing other people's figures inside a walking guest's session, scanning the home again to show what changed, and changing how notes are stored, left or read.

Decisions

  1. asked2026-10-25

    Asked where the scan-over-the-room view should live → chose the note screen gains it, so there is one session, one line-up, and a note can be seen landing on the thing it describes.

  2. asked2026-10-25

    Asked how much of the real room should show through → chose a fade slider from the camera alone to the scan alone, because lining up by eye needs both halves of the misalignment visible.

  3. asked2026-10-25

    Asked again, after the slider: chose one button that turns the scan on and off instead, starting off. Flicking between the real wall and the scanned wall shows a misalignment at least as plainly as blending them, and it is one press with a thumb while walking rather than a drag.

  4. asked2026-10-25

    Asked how the two floors should behave once one is lined up → chose drawing the whole scan, so the floor above or below sits where it really is rather than being hidden.

  5. assumed2026-10-25

    Assumed: Three.js owns the session through renderer.xr.setSession, and the scan is moved to meet the session's origin rather than the camera being moved to meet the scan, because an XR camera is driven by the phone and cannot be set from the page.

  6. assumed2026-10-25

    Assumed: the existing marked spots and toScanPose scale carry the placing, with no new alignment gesture, because the spots are already taped and the same numbers must place the view and the notes or they will disagree.

  7. assumed2026-10-25

    Assumed: a frame-rate floor drops the drawing and leaves the note screen working, rather than letting the page stutter, because a stuttering overlay is worse than none while a note is being left.

  8. log2026-10-25

    Found while planning: the installed Spark already reads renderer.xr.isPresenting and draws with renderer.xr.getCamera(), so splats in an XR session is a supported path rather than a hack.

  9. log2026-10-25

    Checked on the owner's Android phone (Chrome 155): handing the session to renderer.xr works. The overlay still reads screen and takes presses, the first drawn frame arrived after 145 empty ones, and all four marked spots placed the scan without a reload. So the shape of this change is confirmed on the device it has to run on.

  10. log2026-10-25

    Measured on that phone, and it changes the plan: the whole scan drawn at full detail runs at 2 to 66 frames a second and loses the 3D view altogether, against 88 to 90 with it off. So drawing all 546,977 splats is not an option, and the trimmed scan stops being a possible follow-on and becomes part of this change.

  11. log2026-10-25

    Found while reading Spark, and the likely answer: Spark has a level-of-detail system with a splat budget, and its own default budget for WebXR is 500–750k — but it only applies when a mesh asks for lod, which ours never did, so the phone was drawing every splat with no culling. The next run steps through four settings (no LOD, then 250k, 120k and 60k budgets with fewer pixels) and keeps the frame rate for each, so the setting is chosen on evidence rather than guessed.

  12. log2026-10-25

    Found on the same run: four frames in five answered our own getViewerPose with nothing while three.js held the session. The page should take its pose from the camera three.js has already moved, rather than asking the frame a second time — the next run confirms that camera is moving on those frames.

  13. log2026-10-25

    Measured while planning: the scan is 12.5 MB and came off Cloudflare's edge at 268 KB/s, so it takes about three quarters of a minute to arrive before anything can be drawn. So the session starts and tracks while the scan is still downloading, with the progress said out loud, and the trimmed copy of the scan is wanted for the wait as well as for the frame rate.

  14. asked2026-10-25

    Asked whether the copied AR session lifecycle in the two phone screens should be its own change or a step in this one → chose a step in this one, because both would edit the same screen and this change reshapes it anyway.

  15. log2026-10-25

    Found by /improve-code: the two phone screens each write the same AR session lifecycle by hand, the copies have already drifted, and no check can see it. It is now the step before the drawing is added, with today's tests as the pin and an undo if neither screen reads more plainly.

  16. asked2026-10-25

    Asked what moving the device should do for somebody who joined a room on a phone → chose real walking through an AR session, over the cheaper turn-to-look from the phone's orientation sensors. Walking is the stronger moment; the cost is that it is Android only and rests on this change's splat budget to run at all.

  17. asked2026-10-25

    Asked what phones without AR get, every iPhone among them → chose a thumb pad for walking, and no use of their sensors. So looking stays a drag everywhere, and the pad is the one movement control every touch screen has.

  18. asked2026-10-25

    Asked how far one real step should carry you in the scan → chose life size, with a press to carry on walking when the real floor runs out. A step that covered several metres would make the home feel wrong, and the press costs one button.

  19. asked2026-10-25

    Asked how strong the laptop's head reading should be → chose subtle parallax: leaning shifts the view a little, like a window, and the keys and mouse still do the travelling. Amplified looking was the alternative and was rejected as twitchy; leaning to walk as tiring and easy to trigger by accident.

  20. asked2026-10-25

    Asked whether the guest's walk-through should be its own change or part of this one → chose part of this one, because it needs this change's session handling, splat budget and shared scan loading, and a separate plan would sit blocked behind it.

  21. assumed2026-10-25

    Assumed: carrying on walking keeps where you are in the scan and makes where you now physically stand the new starting point, rather than returning you to the middle of the home, because the point of the press is to cross a house two metres at a time without losing your place. The camera choices already there are how somebody jumps across the home deliberately.

  22. assumed2026-10-25

    Assumed: the scan starts drawn for a guest, where it starts off for somebody standing in the real home, because a guest turning it off would be left looking at their own living room. For the same reason a frame rate a guest's phone cannot hold ends the session and returns them to the ordinary page view, rather than leaving the camera's picture behind it.

  23. assumed2026-10-25

    Assumed: a leaning head moves the drawn view only, and the place sent to everybody else stays the one the keys and the pad put you at, because a head wobble relayed ten times a second would make a figure jitter for everyone watching.

  24. assumed2026-10-25

    Assumed: the thumb pad feeds the same movement the keys produce rather than its own path through the camera, because two ways to move one camera is how they drift apart.

  25. log2026-10-25

    Evidence gap, named rather than assumed away: nothing here has measured what reading a face costs beside the splats on a laptop, or what the guest walk-through holds on a phone. Both get a measured probe before they are built, and the laptop's leaning is dropped rather than shipped slow if the cost is too high.

  26. log2026-10-25

  29. log2026-10-25

    Changed while building, in pov-controls.update: the movement vector is scaled by its own length capped at one, rather than always normalised. A key is all or nothing so nothing about the keyboard changes, and a half-pulled pad now walks at half speed instead of full.

  30. log2026-10-25

    Measured on the owner's own laptop, and it contradicts this plan: the scan at today's viewer settings draws at 2 to 28 frames a second, where the design said the laptop holds ninety. That number came from a different machine. So "the laptop keeps today's full-detail path" is withdrawn.

  31. asked2026-10-25

    Asked to fix it, and chose a frame-rate watch that steps down through named settings at runtime over a measured constant for the laptop. Three machines are known and they differ by about forty times, so any single constant is wrong for two of them; the cost is that a weak machine's view softens without being asked, which is what a stutter already does less kindly.

  32. assumed2026-10-25

    Assumed: the softening never climbs back up, because a view flickering between two settings is harder to look at than a softer one that holds, and the person never chose either.

  33. log2026-10-25

    Found while building: the top step's numbers are Spark's own defaults (maxStdDev of Math.sqrt(8), lodRenderScale of 1), so a machine that holds the floor draws exactly what it drew before this change.

  34. assumed2026-10-25

    Assumed: the splat mesh now always asks for the level-of-detail tree, even on a machine that never uses it, because turning it on later would mean loading the 11 MB file again. What that costs at load is unmeasured and is checked on the walk.

  35. log2026-10-25

    Measured on the laptop, first pass: a face read took 289 ms on average against the 66 ms it needs, and drawing plus face plus hands fell to 0–7 fps. Not yet conclusive, because the drawing was itself at 2–28 fps and may have been starving the reader; a second pass with the drawing paused separates the two. Leaning stays unbuilt until it answers.

  36. log2026-10-25

    Built sections 3, 5 and 10 on the phone's measured setting: the placing transform, the session view, and the guest's walk with Carry on from here and Stop. The phone draws at the table's softest step, which is the 60k setting the probe chose.

  37. log2026-10-25

    Found while building: a guest's session must ask for a DOM overlay of its own. Without one the session owns the whole screen and Carry on and Stop are invisible, so the walk could only be left by ending the session from the phone's own gesture.

  38. log2026-10-25

    Changed while building: the walk is offered wherever the phone can track, in a room or walking the scan alone, because the page keeps where the walker stands either way. The room viewer is now always told that place rather than only inside a room, which is one condition fewer and what the lone walker needs to start from. App.test.tsx's expectation that walking alone reports nothing was updated to match, with the reason in the test.

  39. log2026-10-25

    Removed while building, after coverage showed them dead: two "is the view still here" guards in ar-view. Disposing detaches both the lost-context listener and the frame loop, so neither could ever run afterwards, and a guard no test can reach is a guard that is lying.

  40. log2026-10-25

    Measured on the owner's phone, and it settles the open question: the scan ships as it is. With Spark's LOD asked for and the renderer's enableLod set, a 60,000-splat budget at 0.6 pixel ratio and lodRenderScale 4 holds 65–80 fps — the same range the phone reaches drawing nothing. 120k at ¾ pixels holds 25–64, and 250k 60–62. So the budget is the lever, the aggressive setting is the one to ship, and no trimmed copy of the file is needed: the LOD tree is computed in a worker from the same room.spz in 4–5 seconds, so nothing changes about what is served.

  41. log2026-10-25

    Found on the same run, and worth a guard rather than a shrug: the 3D view was still lost at the 250k setting, after three LOD trees had been built in one session. Production builds one setting once and never switches, so this is probably the probe's own churn — but the webglcontextlost path stays in the plan and the renderer keeps only one mesh at a time, because a phone that lost the context three runs out of three is not a phone to take chances with.

  42. log2026-10-25

Reading renderer.xr.getCamera() is now evidence, not a hunch, and asking the frame a second time is the thing not to build.

  43. asked2026-10-25

    Asked, and it reverses this change's own premise: the phone standing in the real home does not need the scan drawn over the real room. It needs to be a camera and a position, because whoever is in the home can already see the home, and the person who joined from somewhere else is the one the scan exists for. So the drawing on the note screen, the on-and-off button, the markers over the real room and the lifecycle refactor that was groundwork for them are all dropped, and Leave.tsx stays as it is. What survives is movement for whoever is away: the pad, the frame rate, and a guest's own walk.

  44. asked2026-10-25

  45. log2026-10-25

Phone.tsx opens the camera first and then starts the session, which may survive, but nobody has checked whether the pictures keep coming. A probe is on the owner's phone now.

  46. log2026-10-25

They were reconciled into the main specs at an earlier checkpoint and described behaviour nobody will now build, so they are gone from roam-ar-view and roam-capture rather than left as a promise.

  47. log2026-10-25

With the front camera opened first and an immersive-ar session started over it, the camera stayed live, not muted and enabled for the whole 32-second run, sending 957 pictures, while 919 of 957 frames carried a pose and 375 showed the phone had moved. So streaming the camera and reporting where it stands do not fight each other, Phone.tsx's existing order is right, and nothing about the person in the real home needs building.

  48. log2026-10-25

    Found in the same run, and it sharpens the earlier warning rather than contradicting it: when the page owns the frame loop and attaches its own layer, only 4% of frames came back without a pose, against 92% when three.js held the session. So getViewerPose is reliable on the path Phone.tsx and Leave.tsx use, and the camera three.js has moved is the thing to read only inside a drawing session.
